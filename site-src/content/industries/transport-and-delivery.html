What the repetitive work looks like in transport and delivery

For a courier contractor, a small freight operator, a removals company or a local delivery business, the day starts before the first van leaves the yard. Orders have come in overnight by email, phone and spreadsheet. Someone has to sort them into runs, send each driver their list and answer the inevitable messages: the address is wrong, the customer is not home, the gate code is missing.

At the other end of the day come the signatures and photos that prove each delivery happened, scattered across drivers' phones. Then the fuel receipts on the dashboard and the mileage that nobody wrote down, which matter when it is time to invoice customers and talk to your accountant.

All of this is repetitive and follows clear rules, which makes it a good fit for automation.

Run sheets and driver messages

  1. Orders land in one list. Jobs arriving by email, web form or a customer's spreadsheet are pulled into a single job list, with addresses tidied and missing details flagged.
  2. Runs are put together. Jobs are grouped by area, time window and vehicle, using the rules your dispatcher already follows. The dispatcher checks and adjusts.
  3. Drivers get their sheet. Each driver receives their run on their phone, with addresses, contact numbers, access notes and special instructions.
  4. Changes reach the right person. If a job is added or cancelled during the day, only the affected driver is told, and customers can be sent an on-the-way message.

Filing proof of delivery

Proof of delivery is only useful if you can find it. In a typical setup the driver opens the job on their phone at the drop-off, takes a photo, captures a signature or notes where the parcel was left, and taps done. The record is saved against the job with the time and driver's name, filed in the customer's folder, and the customer can be sent a delivery confirmation. When someone rings to say a delivery never arrived, the photo is a search away rather than somewhere in a driver's camera roll. Completed jobs can also flow through to invoicing, so fewer deliveries go unbilled.

Agree what counts as proof. Decide with your customers whether a photo, a signature or both is required, and for which jobs. Clear rules make the automation simpler and disputes easier to settle.

Fuel and mileage logs

  1. Receipts are captured on the spot. Drivers photograph fuel receipts, or fuel card statements are picked up from email.
  2. Kilometres are recorded with each run. Odometer readings at the start and end of a shift, or distances from the run sheet, are logged per vehicle.
  3. Everything is matched. Fuel spend and kilometres are matched to vehicles and drivers, and entered as expenses in Xero.
  4. You get a monthly summary. Cost per vehicle and per kilometre, with any unusual figures flagged for a look.

If your vehicles pay road user charges, accurate kilometres make those easier to keep track of too. How these records are used for tax and charges is one to check with your accountant.

Other transport tasks worth automating

  • Customer delivery bookings taken through a web form and added straight to the job list.
  • Invoices created from completed and proven deliveries.
  • Reminders for vehicle servicing, warrants or certificates of fitness, and registration renewals.
  • Driver hours collected from run sheets and totalled for payroll.
  • Damage and incident reports captured with photos at the scene.
  • Answers to where-is-my-delivery questions sent automatically from the job status.
