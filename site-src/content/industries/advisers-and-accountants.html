What the repetitive work looks like in an advisory practice

Accountants, bookkeepers, financial advisers, mortgage and insurance advisers all share a similar week. Part of it is the work clients value: reviewing their position, explaining options, making recommendations. The rest is administration that has to be done properly but does not need your expertise.

Most of that administration falls into three buckets. Chasing clients for the documents you need, sometimes several emails for one set of bank statements. Writing up file notes after each call or meeting, usually at the end of a long day. And keeping compliance records and checklists up to date so that you can show what was done, and why, if someone asks.

In busy periods, such as the run-up to tax deadlines or a wave of refinancing, this admin grows faster than the advice work. That is where automation helps most.

Chasing client documents

  1. A checklist per client. When a job starts, such as an annual return or a new loan application, the client gets a list of exactly what you need and a secure way to upload it.
  2. Uploads are recognised. As files arrive, they are named, filed in the right client folder and ticked off the list.
  3. Reminders only ask for what is missing. On a schedule you set, the client gets a friendly note listing the items still outstanding.
  4. You see the status at a glance. A simple view shows which clients are complete, which are waiting and which need a phone call.

File notes written from calls

With the client's knowledge, a call or meeting is recorded or transcribed. AI then drafts a file note in your usual structure: who was present, what was discussed, the client's goals and circumstances, any advice given and agreed next steps. The draft goes to the adviser to review, correct and approve, and only then is it saved to the client file. Action items can become tasks with due dates. You stay responsible for the content; the automation saves the typing and the blank page.

Settle the privacy questions first. Before recording any calls, decide how clients are told, where recordings and transcripts are stored, and how long they are kept. Our guide to the Privacy Act 2020 and automation covers what to check.

Compliance records and checklists

Good records are mostly about consistency. A typical setup builds your checklist into the workflow, so each step, such as identity checked, disclosure provided, advice recorded or sign-off received, is ticked off as it happens, with the date and who did it. Supporting documents are attached automatically. Missing steps are flagged before a file is closed, and records are kept in a structure that makes them easy to retrieve later. Businesses generally need to keep business records for seven years, so a tidy, searchable structure pays off over time. What your own obligations are is a question for your professional body or compliance adviser.

Other tasks worth automating

  • New client onboarding: engagement letters, identity documents and initial questionnaires sent and tracked.
  • Annual review reminders scheduled for each client.
  • Bank feeds and receipts in Xero checked for missing items before month end.
  • Client emails sorted by client and topic, with routine questions drafted for reply.
  • Weekly work-in-progress summaries showing jobs waiting on clients versus jobs waiting on you.
  • Unbilled time and overdue fees flagged each Monday.

Tools we usually connect for advisers

  • Microsoft 365: Outlook for client email and calendar, Excel for checklists and registers, Teams for internal messages, and OneDrive or SharePoint for client folders.
  • Google Workspace: Gmail and Google Calendar, Google Sheets for checklists and registers, and Google Drive for client folders.
  • Xero for your own billing and, for accountants, client ledgers where access allows.
