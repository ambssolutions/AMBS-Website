Why no-shows happen

Many no-shows are not rude customers. They are busy people who forgot, mixed up the day, could not find the message with the address, or meant to cancel and never got round to calling. If cancelling means ringing during work hours and waiting on hold, many people simply do not bother.

That is good news, because forgetting and friction are both things a system can help with. A clear confirmation, a reminder at the right moment and an easy way to say "I can't make it" can turn some no-shows into either an attended appointment or a cancellation you can fill.

Booking reminders and rebooking are one of the three tasks we usually take off clinics and salons first, with a typical estimate of around three and a half hours a week for the reminding, confirming and rescheduling done by hand. Your own figure might be higher or lower, and the cost of each empty slot matters as much as the admin time.

Start with a good confirmation

The confirmation message, sent as soon as the booking is made, does more work than people expect. It is the message customers search for later when they want to check the time or address.

A good confirmation includes:

  • The day, date and time, written out in full ("Tuesday 13 October at 2:30pm").
  • Who the appointment is with and what it is for.
  • The address, with parking or access notes if they help.
  • Anything the customer needs to bring or do beforehand.
  • How to change or cancel, and how much notice you would like.

If you collect new client forms, the confirmation is also a natural place to send the link, so the paperwork is done before the customer walks in.

Reminder timing that works

There is no single right schedule. The aim is to remind people early enough that you can fill the slot if they cancel, and close enough that they do not forget again. A common starting point looks like this:

MessageWhenPurpose
ConfirmationStraight after bookingAll the details in one place
Main reminderOne to two days beforeConfirm or cancel with enough notice to fill the gap
Same-day nudgeA few hours before, for longer or higher-value appointmentsLast prompt, with the address and a map link
Follow-upAfter the appointmentThank you, aftercare and an invitation to rebook

Adjust the timing to how your customers book. If most bookings are made a month ahead, a reminder a week before can help. If people book the same day, a confirmation may be all they need. Avoid sending messages late at night or very early in the morning, and keep the number of messages sensible. Three reminders for a haircut feels like nagging.

Two-way replies and easy rebooking

A one-way reminder tells people something. A two-way reminder lets them act on it. Asking customers to reply with a simple word, such as YES to confirm or NO to cancel, removes the phone call that stops many people from cancelling properly.

When a reply comes in, the automation can:

  1. Update the booking. Mark it confirmed or cancelled on your booking list, or flag it for your front desk to update in your booking system.
  2. Reply to the customer. A short thank you, or an offer of other times if they cancelled.
  3. Offer the slot to someone else. If the appointment was cancelled, the waitlist is notified.
  4. Pass anything unclear to a person. Replies like "running late, is that OK?" or a question about the treatment go to your front desk to answer.

Customers do not always reply with the exact word you asked for. This is where AI can help, by reading replies like "sorry, can't make it tomorrow" as a cancellation. It should still hand anything uncertain to a person rather than guess. Our guide to AI customer replies covers how that handover works.

Rebooking matters just as much as cancelling. A message after the appointment that says "it's usually about six weeks between visits, would you like to book now?" can bring people back without anyone picking up the phone.

Filling gaps with a waitlist

A cancellation the day before is only a loss if the slot stays empty. A simple waitlist turns it into an opportunity. When someone cancels, the system messages the people waiting for that kind of appointment, and the first to reply gets the slot. Everyone else gets a polite note that it has been filled.

Keep the rules simple: who is eligible for which appointment types, how much notice you need, and what happens if two people reply at once. A person should be able to override the system at any time.

Missed calls are missed bookings

When you are with a client, the phone goes unanswered. Many callers will not leave a voicemail, and some will ring the next business on the list instead. Missed call follow-ups are another of the tasks we usually take off clinics and salons first.

The idea is simple. When a call is missed during opening hours, the caller receives a short text saying you are with a client, with a link to book online or an offer to call back. The missed call is logged so someone can follow up if the person does not book. It gives a lost enquiry a second chance without interrupting the appointment you are in.

Only text numbers that can receive texts. Landlines and withheld numbers usually cannot get a text, so the follow-up should fall back to a call-back list for a person to work through.

Consent, opt-out and the tone of your messages

Booking reminders use personal information: names, phone numbers, and sometimes the type of appointment. That means the Privacy Act 2020 applies. Tell customers when they book how you will contact them, only use their details for that purpose, and give an easy way to stop messages, such as replying STOP. Keep message content discreet. "Your appointment with Sarah on Tuesday at 2:30pm" is better than naming a medical treatment that could be read on a lock screen. Our guide to the Privacy Act 2020 and automation has more on what to check.

Reminders are about bookings the customer has already made. If you want to send promotions as well, treat that as a separate list with its own consent, and check with your adviser about the rules for marketing messages. This section is general information, not legal advice.

Tone matters too. Write the way your front desk speaks: warm, short and clear. Use the customer's first name, sign off with your business name, and avoid capital letters or warnings in the first reminder. If many of your customers speak another language, messages can be sent in their preferred language. Save firmer wording about cancellation fees for the confirmation, where it is information rather than a telling-off.

Measuring no-shows

You cannot tell whether reminders are working unless you know where you started. Before you switch anything on, count for a few normal weeks:

  • Total appointments booked.
  • No-shows, meaning people who did not turn up and did not tell you.
  • Late cancellations, with less notice than you ask for.
  • Gaps that were filled from cancellations.

Most booking systems can give you these figures, or you can tally them in a spreadsheet. Then compare the same numbers after reminders have been running for a few weeks.
