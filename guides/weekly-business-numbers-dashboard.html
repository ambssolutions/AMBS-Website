Your business numbers, every week

Most owners know roughly how the business is going, but the actual figures sit in several different apps. A short weekly summary puts the handful of numbers that matter in one place, so you can spot a problem while it is still small.

Digital tools7 min readUpdated 4 October 2026
In short
  • Track a handful of numbers that lead to a decision: sales, unpaid invoices, cash, jobs, hours and stock.
  • Every number should come straight from the system that already holds it, not from someone retyping it.
  • Start with a Monday summary on your phone. Add a live dashboard only when you check the numbers during the week.
  • Keep it to one screen, and decide who sees what before you build it.
  • If a number never changes what you do, take it off.

Why a weekly look at the numbers helps

Ask many owners how last week went and you will get a feeling rather than a figure. That is not a criticism. The figures are spread across your accounting software, your point of sale or booking system, a timesheet app and a spreadsheet someone updates when they remember. Pulling them together takes time you do not have, so it happens at the end of the month, or when the accountant asks.

The trouble is that problems grow quietly. An invoice that is a little overdue is a phone call. Leave it for months and it can become a write-off conversation. A short, regular look at the right numbers lets you act while the fix is still easy.

The numbers that usually matter

You do not need dozens of measures. A handful is usually enough, as long as each one leads to a decision. Here is a starting list, with where each number usually comes from.

NumberWhat it tells youWhere it usually comes from
SalesHow busy last week was, compared with the week before and the same week last yearPoint of sale, online shop or accounting software
Unpaid invoicesWho owes you, how much, and how overdueAccounting software such as Xero or MYOB
CashWhat is actually in the bank, and what is due out this weekBank feed in your accounting software
JobsJobs booked, in progress, finished and not yet invoicedJob management app, booking system or a shared spreadsheet
HoursStaff hours worked against what was rostered or quotedTimesheet or rostering app, or payroll
StockItems running low, and stock that is not movingPoint of sale or inventory system, such as Lightspeed or Shopify

Pick the ones that fit your business. A café cares about stock and hours; a consultant may have no stock at all and care far more about jobs not yet invoiced.

The test for every number: if it went up or down sharply, would you do something different this week? If the honest answer is no, it does not belong on the summary.

Get each number from the source

A summary is only useful if you trust it, and you only trust it if nobody is retyping figures into it. The rule is simple: each number should be read automatically from the system that already holds it.

In practice that means connecting your tools so they hand information to each other. Most accounting, point of sale and booking systems allow this through an API, which is a secure doorway one program uses to ask another for data. Where a tool has no such doorway, an exported file or a shared Google Sheets or Excel file can act as the go-between. Our guide to connecting your business apps explains how these connections work and what to check first.

Two things to sort out before you build anything:

  • Agree what each number means. Is "sales" including or excluding GST? Is a job "finished" when the work is done or when it is signed off? Write the definitions down once and stick to them.
  • Find the gaps. If invoices are only entered at the end of the month, a weekly unpaid-invoices figure will be out of date for most of the month. Sometimes the first improvement is a small process change, not a dashboard.

A Monday summary or a live dashboard?

There are two common ways to see your numbers, and they suit different owners.

The weekly summary

A short message arrives on your phone or in your inbox every Monday morning: last week's sales, overdue invoices over a set amount, cash on hand, jobs waiting to be invoiced, hours worked and anything low in stock. It is quick to read. Nobody has to remember to send it, and it lands before the week gets busy.

For many small businesses this is the right place to start. It builds the habit of looking, and it is quick to set up because it only needs to read the numbers once a week.

The live dashboard

A dashboard is a single screen that updates itself as new sales, payments and jobs come in. You open it whenever you want, on your phone or a laptop. It suits businesses where things change during the day, such as a busy shop watching sales by hour, or a transport business watching deliveries completed.

A good rule: if you find yourself wanting to check the Monday numbers on a Wednesday, it is time for a live view. Our websites, portals and dashboards service covers how we build these around the tools you already have.

Keep it to one screen

A common mistake is adding everything. Every extra chart makes the important numbers harder to see. Aim for something you can read on a phone without scrolling much.

  1. Put the decision numbers at the top. Cash, overdue invoices and anything that needs action today.
  2. Show a comparison. A number on its own means little. Show it next to last week, or the same week last year, so you can see the direction.
  3. Flag only what needs attention. Highlight, for example, an invoice that is more than 30 days overdue, or stock below its reorder point, and leave everything else plain.
  4. Link to the detail. The summary should tell you where to look, not try to show every line. A tap through to the accounting software or the job list is enough.
  5. Review it after a month. Remove anything nobody looked at, and add the one number you kept asking about.

Who sees what

Not everyone needs every number, and some numbers should stay private. Decide this up front, because it shapes how the summary is built.

  • The owner usually sees everything, including cash and who owes what.
  • A manager or team leader may see jobs, hours and stock, but not bank balances.
  • Your bookkeeper or accountant may only need the unpaid invoices and a monthly view.
  • Staff might see a team figure, such as jobs completed this week, without any financial detail.

Unpaid invoices and timesheets often include customer and staff names, which is personal information. Keep access to the people who need it, and turn on two-factor authentication for anything that shows financial or personal details. Here is what a Monday summary might hold for different kinds of business. Treat these as starting points, not templates.

Café or takeawayHospitality

Sales by day, staff hours against the roster, supplier invoices due and the items running low.

Trades and buildingTrades

Quotes sent and accepted, jobs finished but not invoiced, and overdue invoices by customer.

Clinic or salonHealth and beauty

Appointments booked for the week ahead, no-shows last week, and gaps in the diary.

Shop or dairyRetail

Sales against last year, best and slowest sellers, and stock below its reorder point.

Adviser or accountantProfessional services

Client files waiting on documents, work in progress not yet billed, and fees overdue.

Transport and deliveryTransport

Deliveries completed, proof of delivery still missing, fuel spend and kilometres travelled.

Avoid vanity numbers

Some numbers feel good but rarely change what you do. Website visits, social media followers and total sales since the business opened are the usual suspects. They are not useless, but they do not belong on a one-screen weekly view unless you act on them.

Watch for these traps as well:

  • Turnover without margin. A record sales week can still lose money if it came from discounts or overtime. If you can, show gross profit alongside sales.
  • Averages that hide problems. A healthy-looking average invoice age can hide one customer who is months behind. List the worst offenders by name instead.
  • Numbers without an owner. Every figure on the summary should have someone who acts when it moves. If nobody would, remove it.

Your accountant can help you pick the few financial measures that matter most for your type of business, and check that the summary agrees with what your accounts show.

Common questions

Do I need new software to get a weekly numbers summary?

Usually not. The figures already live in your accounting software, point of sale, booking system or spreadsheets. A summary simply reads from those tools on a schedule and puts the results in one message or screen.

What is the difference between a weekly summary and a live dashboard?

A weekly summary is a short message, often sent on Monday morning, that shows last week's numbers. A live dashboard is a screen you can open at any time that updates itself as new sales, jobs or payments come in. Many small businesses only need the summary.

Can my staff see the dashboard without seeing the financials?

Yes. A well set up dashboard can show different views to different people, so a team leader might see jobs and hours while only the owner sees cash and unpaid invoices.

How accurate will the numbers be?

They are only as accurate as the systems they come from. If invoices are entered late or stock counts are out of date, the summary will reflect that, which is often useful in itself because it shows where the records need tidying.
