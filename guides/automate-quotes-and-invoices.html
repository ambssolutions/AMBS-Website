How to automate quotes and invoices

Quoting and invoicing are where a lot of small businesses lose their evenings. Most of the steps follow the same pattern every time, which makes them good work to hand to a system, as long as a person still checks the numbers.

Automation8 min readUpdated 4 October 2026
In short
  • Automate the typing, the copying and the chasing. Keep pricing decisions and the final check with a person.
  • Agree your price list and quote template before automating, or the system will copy your inconsistencies.
  • Let your accounting software calculate GST at 15% rather than building the maths into the automation.
  • Payment reminders work best when they are polite, regular and stop automatically once a payment is recorded.
  • Keep quotes, approvals and invoices linked and stored together so you can find them for as long as you need to keep records.

Where the time goes in quoting and invoicing

Ask tradespeople when they write quotes and you will often hear the same answer: at night, at the kitchen table, after a full day on the tools. The work itself is not hard. It is the retyping that wears you down. Measurements from a notebook, a photo of the meter board, a voice memo from the ute, then the same details typed into a quote, then into a job sheet, then again into an invoice.

Each step is small, but together they add up. On our home page we list quotes written up from site notes, job invoices into your accounts and chasing late payments as the three tasks we usually take off trades businesses first. Our typical estimates for those are around four, two and a half and two hours a week respectively. Your numbers will be different, which is why it is worth timing them for a normal week before you change anything.

Most of this work follows the same path every time. That makes it a strong candidate for automation, as our guide on choosing what to automate first explains.

The path from site notes to paid invoice

Before automating anything, it helps to see the whole process on one page. For most service businesses it looks like this:

  1. Capture the job. Notes, photos or a voice memo taken on site or during a call.
  2. Draft the quote. The notes are turned into line items using your price list, labour rates and standard wording.
  3. Check and send. A person reviews the draft, adjusts anything unusual and sends it to the customer.
  4. Get approval. The customer accepts by email or a link, and the job is marked as approved.
  5. Invoice the work. When the job is done, or at agreed stages, an invoice is created in your accounting software from the approved quote.
  6. Chase payment. Reminders go out on a set schedule until the invoice is paid.
  7. File the records. Quote, approval, invoice and payment are stored together where you can find them later.

Most of these steps involve moving information from one place to another. Only a few involve real judgement. That split is what tells you what to automate.

From voice memo to priced quote

This is where AI is genuinely useful. A voice memo such as "bathroom in Papatoetoe, replace vanity and tap, two metres of new pipe, half a day" can be transcribed and turned into a structured draft: customer, address, scope of work and line items matched to your price list.

For this to work well, the system needs something to match against:

  • A price list with your common materials and services, kept up to date.
  • Your labour rates, call-out fees and any minimum charges.
  • Standard wording for inclusions, exclusions and terms that you already use.
  • A quote template in the format your customers are used to seeing.

The output is a draft, not a sent quote. You open it on your phone, check that the scope and numbers make sense, change anything that needs your judgement and press send. Most of the typing goes, but the decision stays with you.

Tidy your price list first. If three people quote the same job three different ways, an automation will faithfully copy all three. Agree one price list and one template before you build anything.

Approval and invoicing in your accounting software

Once a customer approves a quote, the rest is mostly bookkeeping. An approval can update the job status, notify whoever schedules the work and, when the job is complete, create an invoice from the approved line items.

We recommend creating invoices directly in your accounting software rather than in a separate system. Xero is the accounting software we connect to. That way your accountant sees everything in the usual place, and your usual bank reconciliation routine does not need to change. Many businesses start with the invoice created as a draft, so someone glances over it before it is approved and sent. Once you trust it, you can decide whether some job types go out automatically.

Let the accounting software handle GST

GST in New Zealand is 15%, and most accounting software already calculates it from the tax settings on each item or account. The safest approach is for the automation to send the line items and let the software do the tax maths, rather than calculating GST separately and risking two different answers. If you are unsure whether your items are set up correctly, check with your accountant before switching anything on. This is general information, not tax advice.

Staged and progress invoices

Longer jobs often need a deposit, progress payments or a final invoice for variations. These can be automated too, but agree the rules clearly first: what percentage at which stage, and who confirms that a stage is complete.

Chasing late payments without the awkward calls

Chasing money is the job nobody wants. It is also one of the easiest to automate well, because the rules are simple and the cost of forgetting is real.

WhenWhat is sentTone
A few days before the due dateA friendly note with the invoice and a payment linkHelpful, not a reminder of debt
On or just after the due dateA reminder that the invoice is now duePolite and matter-of-fact
A week or two overdueA firmer reminder that asks the customer to get in touch if there is a problemClear, still courteous
Further overdueAn alert to you, so a person makes the callPersonal, handled by you

The timing above is an example, not a rule. Set it to match your payment terms and your customers. The two things that matter most are that reminders stop once the payment is recorded, and that the last step is a person, not another automated email.

Watch for part payments and disputes. If a customer has paid some of the invoice or raised a concern, reminders should pause until someone has looked at it. Few things annoy a customer more than an automated "you are overdue" message after a customer has already called to discuss the bill.

What to automate and what to keep human

A useful way to decide is to ask whether the step needs judgement or just accuracy.

  • Automate: transcribing notes, matching items to your price list, filling in customer details, creating draft quotes and invoices, sending reminders on schedule, updating job status, filing documents.
  • Keep human: pricing unusual work, approving quotes before they go out, deciding on discounts, handling disputes, making the final call on serious debts, and talking to customers who are unhappy.

This is the same pattern we use across all our AI automation work: the system prepares, a person decides.

Keeping records you can find later

In New Zealand, businesses generally need to keep their business records for seven years. Automation can make this much easier, because every quote, approval and invoice can be saved and linked as it happens, rather than sitting in someone's inbox or a folder on one laptop.

A good setup keeps the quote, the customer's approval, any variations, the invoice and the payment together, ideally attached to the customer or job in your accounting software or job system. Supplier bills for materials can be picked up from your email, entered as draft bills in Xero and linked to the job. If you are not sure exactly what you need to keep, your accountant can tell you.

Customer details are personal information, so the usual privacy care applies: store them somewhere secure, limit who can see them and know where they are hosted. Our guide to the Privacy Act 2020 and automation covers the basics.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Automating before the process is agreed. Settle on one template, one price list and one set of payment terms first.
  • Sending quotes without a check. Start with drafts. You can loosen the controls later once you trust the results.
  • Building a separate invoicing system. Your accounting software should stay the place where invoices live. Our guide on connecting your business apps explains how to keep one source of truth.
  • Reminders that never stop. Make sure payment and dispute status are checked before every reminder goes out.
  • No owner. Someone in the business should be the person who notices if quotes stop arriving or invoices are not created.

Common questions

Can an automation set my prices?

It can apply the prices you already use, such as your labour rate, call-out fee and standard materials list. Deciding what to charge for unusual work should stay with you, so most setups produce a draft quote that a person checks before it goes out.

Will automated payment reminders annoy my customers?

They usually do not when the wording is polite, the timing is reasonable and the reminders stop once the payment is recorded. Many customers simply forget, and a friendly reminder with a payment link is easier for them than a phone call.

Do I need new accounting software to automate invoicing?

Usually not. We connect to Xero and create invoices there, so your accountant sees everything in the usual place. If you use other accounting software, we do not connect to it directly, so we will talk through in the free session what is possible.

How is GST handled when invoices are created automatically?

The automation sends the line items to your accounting software, and the software applies GST at 15% using the tax settings you already have. Check with your accountant that those settings are right for your business before you switch anything on.

What if a customer wants changes after accepting a quote?

The automation should treat a changed quote as a new version that needs approval again. A person updates the details, the customer approves the new version, and only then does the job move on to invoicing.
