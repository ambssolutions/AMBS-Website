Automation glossary

Automation and AI come with a lot of jargon. Here are the terms you are most likely to hear, explained simply, with a note on why each one matters to a small business.

Getting started8 min readUpdated 4 October 2026
In short
  • You do not need to know these terms to automate, but they help you ask better questions.
  • Most automations come down to a trigger, some steps and an action, joined up by an API or webhook.
  • AI tools can draft and summarise, but a person should check anything that matters.
  • Security and privacy terms, such as two-factor authentication and personal information, are worth knowing well.

How to use this glossary

Terms are listed alphabetically and grouped by letter. Each one has a short, simple meaning and, where it helps, an example from a typical New Zealand small business. If a term you have heard is not here, ask whoever is building your system to explain it. A good builder will happily do that without making you feel silly. Where an entry touches on law or tax, it is general information, not legal or tax advice.

The three words that explain most automations: a trigger starts it, a workflow sets out the steps, and an integration lets your apps pass information along the way.

A–B

AI agent
An AI system that can carry out tasks, not just answer questions. For example, it might read a booking request, check the calendar and offer a time. It should work within clear limits, with a person able to step in.
API
Short for application programming interface. It is a secure doorway that lets one program ask another for information or send it some. Most modern business software, such as Xero, Microsoft 365 and Google Workspace, has one, and it is what makes most app connections possible.
Approval step
A point in an automation where it pauses for a person to check and confirm before carrying on. A drafted quote waiting for your OK before it goes to the customer is a typical example.
Automation
Getting software to do a repetitive task that a person used to do by hand, following the same rules every time. Sending booking reminders or turning an approved job into an invoice are common examples. Our guide on choosing what to automate first covers where to start.
Backup
A separate copy of your data that you can restore if the original is lost, deleted or damaged. A good backup is stored somewhere different from the original and is tested now and then.
Bank feed
A connection that brings your bank transactions into your accounting software automatically, so they do not need to be typed in. It is usually the starting point for matching payments to invoices.

C

Chatbot
A program that answers messages in a chat window, such as one on your website. Simple chatbots follow fixed scripts; AI chatbots can understand questions written in everyday language. Either way, there should be an easy route to a real person.
Cloud software
Software you use through a web browser or app, with your data stored on the provider's servers rather than on your own computer. Xero, Gmail and Google Sheets are all cloud software.
CRM
Short for customer relationship management. A CRM is a system that keeps all your customer details, enquiries, notes and history in one place. There is plenty of dedicated CRM software, though a well organised spreadsheet in Excel or Google Sheets can do the job for a very small business.
CSV file
A simple text file of data in rows and columns, separated by commas. Most software can export and import CSV files, which makes them a handy go-between when two tools cannot connect directly.

D–G

Dashboard
A single screen that shows your key numbers, such as sales, unpaid invoices and jobs, and updates itself as things change. Our guide to your business numbers every week explains what to put on one.
Data mapping
Working out which piece of information in one app matches which piece in another. For example, deciding that "Client name" in your booking system goes into "Contact" in your accounting software. Getting this right is a big part of any integration.
Field
One piece of information in a form or record, such as a name, phone number or invoice date. Automations move information field by field.
Google Business Profile
Google's free listing for your business, which can appear in Google Maps and local search results. It shows your hours, location, phone number, photos and reviews, and it is one of the most useful things a local business can keep up to date.
GST
Goods and Services Tax, New Zealand's 15% tax on most goods and services. Automations that create quotes or invoices need to handle GST correctly. Your accountant can confirm how it applies to your business.

H–N

Hallucination
When an AI tool states something that sounds confident but is wrong or made up. It is the main reason a person should check AI-written content before it goes to a customer.
Hosting
Where a website, app or data actually lives: the servers that store it and keep it running. Hosting location matters for speed and for privacy. Data we store for clients is hosted in New Zealand or Australia.
Human in the loop
A design where a person reviews or approves key steps of an automated process rather than letting it run entirely on its own. It is sensible wherever a mistake would be costly or embarrassing.
Integration
A connection between two pieces of software so they share information automatically. For example, an integration might turn each approved quote into a draft invoice in Xero. Our guide to connecting your business apps covers how they work.
LLM
Short for large language model. It is the type of AI behind most modern chat and writing tools. It has learned patterns from a huge amount of text, which lets it understand questions and write replies, summaries and drafts.
Low-code
Tools that let you build apps or automations mostly by dragging and configuring, with a little code where needed. They sit between no-code tools and fully custom software.
Multi-factor authentication
The general name for logins that need more than one proof of identity. Two-factor authentication is the most common kind.
No-code
Tools that let people build automations, forms or simple apps without writing any code, usually by choosing steps from menus. They are great for simple jobs, but complex processes can outgrow them.
Notifiable privacy breach
A privacy breach that has caused, or is likely to cause, serious harm to someone. Under the Privacy Act 2020 you must tell the Privacy Commissioner and the people affected as soon as practicable.

O–S

OCR
Short for optical character recognition. It is software that reads text from a photo or scanned document, such as a supplier invoice or a receipt, and turns it into data a computer can use. Receipt and bill capture tools use it to pull details from documents.
Permissions
Settings that control who can see or change what in a system. Good permissions mean each person sees only what their role needs.
Personal information
Any information about an identifiable person, such as a name, phone number, address, health details or booking history. The Privacy Act 2020 sets the rules for collecting, storing and using it.
Portal
A private website or app where staff or customers log in to see and do things, such as track a job, upload photos, approve a quote or download an invoice. It is your own app on every phone, without an app store.
Privacy Act 2020
New Zealand's main privacy law. It is built around 13 information privacy principles covering how personal information is collected, stored, used, shared and deleted. Our guide to the Privacy Act and automation explains what to check.
Prompt
The instructions or question you give an AI tool. Clear prompts, with examples and limits, usually get more reliable results.
Scheduled task
An automation that runs at a set time rather than in response to an event. A sales summary sent every Monday at 7am is a scheduled task.
SEO
Short for search engine optimisation. It is the work of helping search engines understand your website so it shows up when people search for what you offer. For local businesses, it starts with clear pages and a complete Google Business Profile.
Single source of truth
One agreed place where a piece of information is kept and updated, with every other system reading from it. If customer details live only in your CRM and flow out from there, you avoid three slightly different versions.
SSL and HTTPS
The security that encrypts information travelling between a website and a visitor's browser. A site using it has an address starting with https://, and browsers warn visitors when a site does not use it. Every website with a contact form should have it.
Sync
Short for synchronise. It means keeping the same information up to date in two or more places, so a change in one shows up in the others.

T–Z

Template
A reusable layout for a document or message, with gaps the automation fills in, such as the customer's name and appointment time in a reminder text.
Training data
The information used to teach an AI model. Some AI providers use what customers send them to train future models, so check the settings and terms before sharing business data. Data we handle for clients is never used to train anyone's AI model, and the AI tools we use on client data run on business plans that do not train on it.
Trigger
The event that starts an automation. A new booking, an email arriving, a form being submitted or a job being marked complete are all common triggers.
Two-factor authentication
A login that needs your password plus a second check, usually a code from your phone or an authenticator app. It makes it much harder for someone with a stolen password to get in, and it is worth turning on for email, accounting and banking.
Webhook
A message one app sends to another the moment something happens, such as "a new order has just come in". It lets automations react straight away rather than checking for changes every few minutes.
Workflow
The series of steps a task goes through from start to finish, whether done by people, software or both. Writing the workflow down is usually the first step in automating it.

Common questions

What is the difference between automation and AI?

Automation follows set rules: when this happens, do that. AI can handle less predictable work, such as reading an email and drafting a reply. Many useful systems combine the two, with plain automation doing most of the work and AI helping at one or two steps.

Do I need to understand APIs to automate my business?

No. It helps to know that an API is how two programs exchange information, because it explains why some tools connect easily and others do not. The person building your automation should handle the technical detail and explain it in plain words.

Is a chatbot the same as an AI agent?

Not quite. A chatbot answers messages in a conversation. An AI agent can also take actions, such as checking a calendar and making a booking. Both should have clear limits and a way to hand over to a person.
