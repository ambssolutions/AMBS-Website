How to reduce no-shows with booking reminders

An empty chair at 2:30 is time you can never sell again. The fix is rarely a stricter cancellation policy. It is a few well-timed messages, and an easy way for people to say "I can't make it".

In short
  • Send a confirmation straight away and at least one reminder before the appointment, timed to how far ahead people book.
  • Make reminders two-way so customers can confirm, cancel or rebook with a simple reply.
  • Use cancellations to fill gaps from a waitlist instead of leaving the slot empty.
  • Only send reminders to people who have agreed to hear from you, and make opting out easy.
  • Measure your no-show rate for a few weeks before and after, so you know whether it is working.

The 2:30 that never turns up

Picture a Tuesday afternoon at a busy salon. The chair is ready, the colour is mixed, and the 2:30 doesn't arrive. No call, no text. By 2:45 everyone knows the slot is gone.

Here's the thing: most no-shows aren't rude customers. They're busy people who forgot, mixed up the day, couldn't find the message with the address, or meant to cancel and never got round to calling. If cancelling means ringing during work hours and waiting on hold, plenty of people simply don't bother.

That's actually good news. Forgetting and friction are both things a system can fix. A clear confirmation, a reminder at the right moment and an easy way to say "I can't make it" turn many no-shows into either an appointment that happens or a cancellation you can fill.

Worth knowing. Reminders and rebooking are one of the three tasks we usually take off clinics and salons first. Our typical estimate is around three and a half hours a week of reminding, confirming and rescheduling done by hand. Your own figure may be higher or lower, and each empty slot costs as much as the admin time.

Message one: the confirmation

The confirmation goes out the moment the booking is made, and it works harder than you'd think. It's the message people scroll back to when they want to check the time or find the address.

Hi Mia, you're booked in with Sarah on Tuesday 13 October at 2:30pm for a cut and colour. We're on Rata Street, with parking behind the building. Need to change? Just reply here, ideally a day's notice. See you then! – Studio Bloom

Notice what it covers:

  • The day, date and time, written out in full.
  • Who the appointment is with and what it's for.
  • The address, with parking or access notes if they help.
  • Anything to bring or do beforehand.
  • How to change or cancel, and how much notice you'd like.

If new clients fill in a form, put the link in the confirmation too. The paperwork is done before they walk in.

When to send the reminders

There's no single perfect schedule. You want reminders early enough that you can fill the slot if someone cancels, and close enough that they don't forget again. Most businesses start with something like this:

MessageWhenWhy
ConfirmationStraight after bookingAll the details in one place
Main reminderOne to two days beforeConfirm or cancel with enough notice to fill the gap
Same-day nudgeA few hours before, for longer or higher-value appointmentsLast prompt, with the address and a map link
Follow-upAfter the appointmentThank you, aftercare and an invitation to rebook

Then tune it to your customers. If most people book a month ahead, add a reminder a week before. If they book on the day, the confirmation may be all they need. Skip late-night and early-morning sends, and keep the count sensible: three reminders for a haircut feels like nagging.

Let people reply, not just read

A one-way reminder tells people something. A two-way reminder lets them do something about it, right there in the message.

Hi Mia, a reminder of your appointment with Sarah tomorrow at 2:30pm. Reply YES to confirm or NO if you can't make it.

sorry cant make it tomorrow, can i do next week?

No problem, thanks for letting us know. Sarah has Tuesday 20th at 11am or Thursday 22nd at 3pm. Which suits?

Mia didn't reply with the exact word you asked for. People rarely do. This is where AI helps: it reads "sorry cant make it" as a cancellation. Anything it isn't sure about goes to a person rather than a guess. Our guide to AI customer replies explains how that handover works.

Behind the scenes, each reply sets off four small jobs:

  1. Update the booking. Mark it confirmed or cancelled on your booking list, or flag it for your front desk to update in your booking system.
  2. Answer the customer. A short thank you, or other times if they cancelled.
  3. Offer the slot to someone else. If it was a cancellation, the waitlist hears about it.
  4. Pass anything unclear to a person. "Running late, is that OK?" or a question about the treatment goes to your front desk.

Rebooking matters as much as cancelling. A message after the visit, "it's usually about six weeks between visits, would you like to book now?", brings people back without anyone picking up the phone.

Turn a cancellation into a booking

A cancellation the day before is only a loss if the slot stays empty. With a simple waitlist, it becomes a second chance.

Good news: a cut and colour spot just opened tomorrow at 2:30pm with Sarah. Reply YES to grab it. First reply gets it.

The first person to reply gets the slot, and everyone else gets a polite note that it's been filled. Keep the rules simple: who's eligible for which appointment types, how much notice you need, and what happens if two people reply at once. A person can always override the system.

The missed call you never heard

You're mid-appointment and the phone rings out. Many callers won't leave a voicemail, and some ring the next business on the list. Missed call follow-ups are another task we usually take off clinics and salons first.

Sorry we missed your call, we're with a client right now. You can book online here: studio.example/book, or reply and we'll call you back.

The missed call is logged too, so someone follows up if the person doesn't book. A lost enquiry gets a second chance, and the appointment you're in isn't interrupted.

Only text numbers that can receive texts. Landlines and withheld numbers usually can't get a text, so those go to a call-back list for a person to work through.

Keep it friendly, private and opt-in

Reminders use personal information: names, phone numbers, sometimes the type of appointment. So the Privacy Act 2020 applies. Tell customers when they book how you'll contact them, use their details only for that, and give an easy way to stop, such as replying STOP. Keep the content discreet: "your appointment with Sarah on Tuesday at 2:30pm" is better than naming a medical treatment that anyone can read on a lock screen. Our guide to the Privacy Act 2020 and automation has more on what to check.

Reminders are about bookings people have already made. Promotions are different: keep them on a separate list with its own consent, and check with your adviser about the rules for marketing messages. This section is general information, not legal advice.

And write the way your front desk talks: warm, short and clear. Use the customer's first name, sign off with your business name, and skip capital letters and warnings in the first reminder. If many customers speak another language, send messages in their preferred one. Firmer wording about cancellation fees belongs in the confirmation, where it reads as information rather than a telling-off.

Know if it's working

You can only tell whether reminders help if you know where you started. Before switching anything on, count these for a few normal weeks:

  • Total appointments booked.
  • No-shows: people who didn't turn up and didn't tell you.
  • Late cancellations, with less notice than you ask for.
  • Gaps that were filled from cancellations.

Most booking systems can give you these numbers, or you can tally them in a spreadsheet. Run the reminders for a few weeks, then compare. Want the figures on your phone every Monday without pulling a report? Our guide to your weekly business numbers shows how.

Common questions

How far ahead should I send appointment reminders?

Many businesses send one reminder a day or two before and, for longer or higher-value appointments, a second one a few hours before. The right timing depends on how far ahead your customers book and how much notice you need to fill a gap, so test it for a few weeks and adjust.

Should reminders be sent by text or email?

Text messages are often read sooner, which makes them a good fit for reminders close to the appointment. Email works well for confirmations that include details like address, parking or preparation instructions. Many businesses use both.

Do I need permission to send booking reminders?

Messages about a booking the customer has made are generally expected, but you should still tell customers how you will contact them, collect their details with that purpose in mind and make it easy to opt out. Marketing messages are a separate matter with stricter expectations, so check with your adviser if you plan to send promotions. This is general information, not legal advice.

What should happen when a customer replies to cancel?

The booking should be marked as cancelled, or flagged for whoever updates your booking system, the customer should get a short reply offering to rebook, and the freed slot should be offered to people on your waitlist. A person should see anything the system cannot understand.
