Websites that turn visitors into calls

A small business website has one main job: help the right people get in touch. That rarely takes clever design. It takes a short list of basics done properly, from load speed to a contact form that actually reaches you.

Digital tools8 min readUpdated 4 October 2026

In short
  • Many visitors arrive on a phone. Design for that first and check every page on a real phone.
  • The first screen should say what you do, where, and how to get in touch, with a tap-to-call button.
  • Contact forms should save every enquiry somewhere safe, not just send an email that can go missing.
  • Local search depends on a complete Google Business Profile and the same name, address and phone everywhere.
  • Measure calls and form enquiries, not just visits, so you know what the website is doing for you.

What a small business website is really for

Picture someone standing at the bus stop with water dripping through their kitchen ceiling at home. They've searched "plumber near me" on their phone, opened your website, and now they're squinting at it, trying to find your number before the bus arrives.

That's who most of your visitors are. They already have a problem they want solved: a burst pipe, a sore back, a tax return, a catering order.

They want to know three things, quickly: do you do this, do you work near me, and how do I reach you? A website that answers those clearly will usually do more for you than one with beautiful pictures and a vague headline.

This guide covers the basics that tend to matter most for New Zealand small businesses. You can check most of them yourself in an afternoon, with your phone in your hand.

Website speed and mobile come first

Many visitors to a local business website arrive on a phone, often on mobile data, often while doing something else. If the page is slow or awkward, they go back to the search results and try the next business.

The good news is that the usual fixes aren't complicated:

  • Smaller images. Photos straight from a camera or phone are often far larger than a web page needs. Resizing and compressing them is often the single biggest speed gain.
  • Fewer add-ons. Every chat widget, slider, pop-up and tracking script adds weight. Keep the ones that earn their place.
  • Readable text without zooming. Body text should be comfortable to read on a small screen, with buttons big enough to tap with a thumb.
  • Test on a real phone. Not just a desktop browser made narrow. Try it on mobile data, not only your office Wi-Fi.

Run a free speed check. Free tools such as Google's PageSpeed Insights will test a page and list what's slowing it down. You don't need to chase a perfect score, but do fix anything it flags as a serious problem.

A first screen that gets you calls

The first screen is what people see before they scroll. On a phone that's not much space, so it needs to work hard. Four jobs, in this order:

  1. Say what you do, in plain words. "Plumber in West Auckland, same-day callouts" beats "Excellence in every drop".
  2. Say where you work. Suburbs, city or region. People want to know you'll come to them.
  3. Give one obvious next step. A tap-to-call button, a booking button or a short form, whichever you most want people to use.
  4. Show one reason to trust you. Years in business, a licence or registration you hold, or a genuine review.

Go back to the person at the bus stop. If those four things are on the first screen, they can ring you before the bus pulls in.

Tap-to-call: your phone number, one tap away

On a phone, your number should be a link that starts a call with one tap. It sounds obvious, but many sites show the number as plain text or inside an image, which forces people to copy it by hand.

Put it in the header so it's visible on every page. And make sure it goes to a phone that someone answers, or to voicemail that someone checks.

If you're often on a job or with a client when the phone rings, missed calls can quietly cost you work. An automatic text back to missed callers can help:

Sorry we missed your call, we're on a job right now. Reply here with what you need and we'll call you back. – Harbour Plumbing (example)

Contact forms that actually reach you

A contact form that quietly fails is worse than no form at all. The customer thinks they've been in touch, and you never hear from them.

Forms fail more often than people expect. Emails land in spam, a mailbox is full, the staff member who received them has left, or a website update breaks the sending.

Test your form today. Fill it in as a customer would, from your phone, and check it arrives. Then do it again once a month. It only takes a moment, and it costs nothing.

A sturdier set-up saves every enquiry somewhere first, then sends the notification:

  • Saved, not just emailed. Each enquiry is stored in a spreadsheet, CRM (customer relationship management system) or your job app, so nothing is lost if an email goes astray.
  • A copy to the customer. A short automatic reply confirming you received it and when they can expect to hear back.
  • Only the fields you need. Name, a way to reply and what they need. Every extra field puts some people off, and it's more personal information to look after.
  • A clear thank-you message on screen, plus a phone number in case they'd rather call.

Here's what that automatic reply might look like from the customer's side:

Hi, my kitchen tap is leaking under the sink. Can someone come out this week? Mere, Henderson

Thanks Mere, we've got your message. We'll be in touch by the end of the next working day. If it's urgent, give us a call.

Once enquiries are saved in one place, they can flow straight into your other tools. Our guide to AI customer replies for small business covers how common questions can be answered quickly while a person still handles the rest.

Pages in your customers' languages

New Zealand's communities speak many languages, and plenty of people feel more confident booking a service or asking about prices in their first language.

If a good share of your customers speak, for example, Chinese, Hindi, Punjabi, Samoan, Tongan or te reo Māori, translated pages can make it much easier for them to get in touch.

You don't need to translate the whole site at once. Start with the pages that lead to an enquiry: your services, prices or how pricing works, opening hours and contact details. A few tips:

  • Have translations checked by a fluent speaker. Automatic translation is a useful first draft, not a finished page.
  • Let people switch language easily from every page, and remember their choice.
  • If you offer a service in another language, say so clearly, and make sure someone can actually reply in it.

Our own website is available in English, Chinese, Hindi, te reo Māori and Punjabi for exactly this reason, and we speak with customers in English, Hindi and Punjabi.

Getting found on Google: local SEO basics

SEO (search engine optimisation) is the work of helping search engines understand your website so it appears when people search. For a local business, most of the value comes from a few basics.

What to doWhy it helps
Complete your Google Business ProfileThis is the free listing that can appear in Google Maps and local results. Fill in your category, service area, hours, photos and a link to your website, and keep it up to date.
Use the same name, address and phone everywhereYour website, Google Business Profile, social media pages and directory listings should match exactly, so search engines are confident they all describe the same business.
Write a clear title and description for each pageThe page title and description are often what show in search results. Put the service and place first, such as "Electrician in Hamilton".
Give each main service its own pageA useful page about each service, with real details, prices or how pricing works, and the areas you cover, matches what people actually search for.
Answer common questionsThe questions customers ask you on the phone are often the questions they type into Google.

No quick magic. Local SEO tends to build over months. Be wary of anyone who promises first place on Google.

Trust signals, without fake reviews

People want some reassurance before they call a business they've never used. Honest trust signals include:

  • Your real name and a photo of you or your team
  • Where you're based and the areas you cover
  • Licences, registrations or memberships you genuinely hold
  • Photos of your actual work, with the customer's permission
  • Genuine reviews, shown as written, or a link to your Google reviews
  • Clear information on how pricing works, even if it's "free quote after a site visit"

Never fake it. Don't write, buy or edit reviews, and don't show badges or awards you haven't earned. Apart from the ethical problem, misleading claims can breach consumer law and search engine policies, and customers often spot them.

Accessibility: a website everyone can use

An accessible website works for people with low vision, colour blindness or shaky hands, and for people who use a screen reader. It also tends to work better for everyone else.

The Web Content Accessibility Guidelines (WCAG) are the usual reference, but you can start with the basics:

  • Strong contrast between text and background
  • Text that can be enlarged without breaking the layout
  • Descriptive alt text on meaningful images, so screen readers can describe them
  • Buttons and links that say what they do, rather than "click here"
  • Forms with clear labels and error messages that explain what to fix

Count the calls, not just the visits

Visits and page views are easy to count, but they don't pay the bills. What you really want to know is how many people got in touch. Track:

  • Taps on your phone number
  • Contact form enquiries received and saved
  • Bookings made through the website
  • How new customers say they found you, asked on the first call

Put these on your weekly summary next to sales and jobs, and you can start to see whether website changes are making a difference. Our guide to your business numbers every week explains how to set that up.

Common questions

How fast should a small business website be?

As fast as you can reasonably make it, especially on a phone with a weak signal. Free tools such as Google's PageSpeed Insights will test your pages and point out what is slowing them down, which is often large images or too many add-ons.

Do I need my website in more than one language?

Not always, but if many of your customers are more comfortable in another language, a few translated pages covering your services, prices and contact details can make it much easier for them to get in touch. Have translations checked by a fluent speaker rather than relying on automatic translation alone.

Is it all right to put reviews on my website?

Yes, as long as they are genuine reviews from real customers, shown as they were written, with permission where needed. Never write or buy reviews. Linking to your Google reviews is a simple, honest option.

How do I know if my website is bringing in enquiries?

Count them. Track how many people tap your phone number and submit your contact form each week, and ask new customers how they found you. Website visits alone do not tell you much.
